# Onboarding

- A new team member should be able to clone the repository, follow the
  README, and have the full local environment running within thirty
  minutes without asking anyone a question. If that stops being true,
  fixing the onboarding path is a higher priority than the next feature,
  because every day it stays broken costs the next new hire the same
  thirty-minute-or-more tax, and that cost compounds across a whole team.
- Pair the new person with someone on their first real pull request, not
  as a formality but because the review comments on that first PR teach
  more about the team's actual conventions than any document could, and
  because a new contributor's first experience of code review sets their
  expectations for every review after it.
- Give every new hire a small, well-scoped, genuinely useful first task
  rather than a toy task invented for the occasion — a real bug fix or a
  small real feature teaches the codebase faster than a synthetic
  exercise, and it gives them something real to point to on day three.
- Keep an up-to-date glossary of internal terms, service names, and
  acronyms; a team that's been together for years stops noticing how much
  shared vocabulary it's accumulated, and that vocabulary is invisible
  to everyone it wasn't built in front of.
- Access requests (repository, cloud console, internal tools) go through
  a single documented checklist, not word-of-mouth. Word-of-mouth access
  provisioning means the new person is blocked exactly as long as it
  takes someone to remember they exist and have five free minutes.
- Schedule a specific day-thirty check-in, not just an open invitation to
  ask questions. An open invitation gets used by people who are already
  comfortable asking questions and ignored by people who most need it,
  which is usually the opposite of where help is most needed.
